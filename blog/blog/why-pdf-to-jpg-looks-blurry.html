Why Your PDF to JPG Conversion Looks Blurry: A Practical Fix Guide

7 min read File Conversion Guides

You open the PDF and it looks perfect. You convert it to JPG, and suddenly the text is soft, the thin lines in the diagram are fuzzy, and small numbers are hard to read.

Nothing is wrong with your file. This is the most common reason a PDF to JPG conversion looks blurry, and it is usually fixable in a minute. This guide explains what is happening and how to choose the right resolution for text pages, scanned pages, and photos.

The Short Answer

A PDF page can store text and diagrams as vector data, which stays sharp at any zoom. A JPG is a grid of pixels with a fixed size. When you convert, the page is turned into that grid at one particular resolution. If the resolution is too low, or the JPG compression is too strong, the result looks blurry.

Most blurry conversions come from one of these:

  • The export resolution (DPI) is too low for the page.
  • The JPG quality is set too low, which smudges text edges.
  • The PDF is a scan, so the original image was already soft.
  • The JPG is being zoomed or printed much larger than its pixel size allows.

Why a PDF Can Look Sharp and a JPG Doesn’t

Think of a PDF as a set of instructions: “draw this letter here, in this font, at this size.” A viewer can redraw those instructions at any zoom level, so edges stay crisp.

A JPG has no instructions. It is a fixed grid of colored pixels. Once the page is converted, you can’t get more detail than the grid holds. Zoom in and you simply see bigger pixels.

Feature PDF (text and diagrams) JPG
How it’s stored Mostly vector instructions Fixed grid of pixels
Zooming in Stays sharp Gets blurry or blocky
Compression Depends on the content Lossy, always
Best for Documents, reading, printing Sharing a page as a picture

Note: A PDF can also contain regular photos or scans. Those are already pixel-based. More on that below.

Reason 1: The Resolution Is Too Low

When a PDF page becomes a JPG, the converter has to decide how many pixels to create. That is what the DPI (dots per inch) or “scale” setting controls. Higher DPI means more pixels, and more pixels mean sharper text.

Here is how the pixel size of a standard page changes with DPI:

DPI A4 page (approx. pixels) US Letter page (approx. pixels) Typical use
72 595 × 842 612 × 792 Very small preview
150 1240 × 1754 1275 × 1650 Reading on screen, sharing
300 2480 × 3508 2550 × 3300 Printing, small text, detail

These are simple calculations (page size in inches × DPI), so they are accurate for standard page sizes. A page converted at 72 DPI has only about a quarter of the pixels of 150 DPI, which is why small text turns to mush.

Some converters use a low default resolution to keep files small, so it’s worth checking what yours uses.

The fix: Increase the resolution, or choose a higher quality option if the tool doesn’t show DPI directly.

Reason 2: The JPG Quality Setting Is Too Low

JPG is lossy. It throws away detail to save space, and text suffers more than photos because letters have sharp edges on a plain background.

Low quality creates a fuzzy halo around letters, often called compression artifacts. You may notice it as speckles or smudges near the text. Colored text on a colored background is even more affected.

The fix: Use a higher quality setting for text pages. Don’t go to the extreme maximum automatically, because the file gets much larger. Test a couple of levels and zoom in to compare.

Reason 3: The PDF Is a Scan

This is the one people don’t expect. A scanned PDF is usually just a photo of a page placed inside a PDF. The text isn’t text at all. It’s pixels.

That means the quality is capped by the original scan. If it was scanned at low resolution, or photographed on a phone in poor light, converting it to JPG at 600 DPI won’t add detail that was never captured. It will only make a bigger file of the same blurry image.

How to tell: Open the PDF and try selecting a word with your cursor. If you can’t select any text, or the page zooms into pixels, it’s probably a scan.

The fix: Convert at a resolution close to the scan’s original, not far above it. If the scan itself is poor, the real fix is to rescan at a higher resolution.

Reason 4: Photos and Diagrams Inside the PDF Are Already Low Quality

A PDF can contain a mix: sharp vector text, plus embedded images. The text will convert well at a good resolution, but a low-quality picture inside the PDF stays low quality.

Charts and diagrams are the tricky part. If a diagram was created as vector, it converts sharply at a high enough DPI. If it was pasted in as a small screenshot, it is already limited by its own pixels.

Reason 5: JPG Is Not Ideal for Text

Even with good settings, JPG isn’t the perfect format for pages full of text, thin lines, and flat colors. PNG is lossless, so it keeps letter edges clean, though the file is often larger.

If you only need to share or upload the page and sharpness matters most, consider PNG for text-heavy pages and JPG for photo-heavy pages. (This links to a question we covered earlier: why image files can get bigger after converting formats.)

Reason 6: The Image Is Being Enlarged

Sometimes the conversion is fine, but the JPG is used in a way that stretches it. A 1240 px wide image placed in a layout that displays it 2000 px wide will look soft, no matter how it was made. Printing works the same way: a low-pixel image printed large looks blurry.

The fix: Convert at a resolution that matches how the image will be used.

Which Resolution Should You Choose? A Decision Guide

There is no single best DPI. It depends on what’s in the page and where the JPG will go.

Your page type Suggested starting point Why Watch out for
Text-heavy page (reports, assignments, contracts) 150 DPI for screen, 300 DPI for print or small text Letters need enough pixels to stay crisp Low JPG quality smudges text
Page with small fonts or tables 300 DPI Tiny characters need more detail Larger file size
Scanned page Close to the scan’s original resolution Can’t add detail that isn’t there Going far above just bloats the file
Photo-heavy page 150–200 DPI is often enough Photos hide detail loss better Don’t exceed the photo’s own resolution
Diagram or chart with thin lines 200–300 DPI Thin lines blur easily Consider PNG
Quick preview or thumbnail 72–100 DPI Small file matters more than detail Not for reading small text

These are starting points, not rules. Always zoom in to 100% and check the result.

What Happens to File Size at Higher Resolution?

Doubling the DPI doesn’t double the file size. It roughly quadruples the number of pixels (twice as wide and twice as tall), and file size tends to grow a lot with it. That’s the trade-off: sharper, but heavier.

If a high-resolution JPG becomes too large to upload or share, compress it afterwards, and compare it again at 100% zoom. Resolution first, then compression.

Test It Yourself

Don’t just trust the table above. Run a quick test on one of your own PDFs:

  1. Pick one page that has text, a small table, and a diagram.
  2. Convert the same page at three resolutions, for example low, medium, and high. Write down the exact settings your tool offers, since different tools give different options.
  3. Keep the JPG quality setting the same for each.
  4. Record the file size and the pixel dimensions.
  5. Zoom to 100% and check the smallest text and the thinnest line.

Step-by-Step: How to Fix a Blurry PDF to JPG

  1. Check what kind of PDF it is. Can you select the text? If yes, it’s a normal PDF. If not, it’s likely a scan.
  2. Raise the resolution to the next level and convert again.
  3. Use a higher quality setting if the tool allows it.
  4. Zoom to 100% and check the smallest text.
  5. Switch to PNG if letters still look smudged and the file size is acceptable.
  6. Compress at the end if the file is too large.

Common Mistakes

  • Judging sharpness at a zoom level that’s different from how it will be used
  • Converting a poor scan at a very high resolution and expecting improvement
  • Using the lowest quality setting to get a small file, then wondering why text is fuzzy
  • Re-saving the JPG again and again (quality drops each time)
  • Using JPG for text-heavy pages when PNG would be cleaner
  • Comparing results without zooming to 100%

Quick Summary

  • PDF text is vector and stays sharp. JPG is pixels and doesn’t.
  • Low DPI and low JPG quality are the two biggest causes of blur.
  • Scanned PDFs are capped by the scan quality.
  • Text pages need more resolution than photo pages.
  • PNG can keep text cleaner than JPG.
  • Higher resolution means a bigger file, so compress afterwards if needed.

Frequently asked questions

What DPI should I use for PDF to JPG?
For screen viewing, 150 DPI is a common starting point. For printing or very small text, 300 DPI is better. Test with your own page, since needs vary.
Why is the text blurry but the PDF looks sharp?
The PDF stores text as vector instructions that redraw at any zoom. The JPG is a fixed grid of pixels, so low resolution or strong compression makes edges soft.
Can I fix a blurry scanned PDF by converting it at a higher DPI?
Not really. Converting at higher DPI can’t add detail the scan never captured. Rescanning at a higher resolution is the real fix.
Is PNG better than JPG for text pages?
Often, yes. PNG is lossless, so text edges stay cleaner. The trade-off is that the files can be larger.
Why did my JPG get so large at higher resolution?
More pixels means more data. Doubling the DPI roughly quadruples the pixel count. Compress the result afterwards if you need a smaller file.
Does JPG quality matter for text?
Yes. Text has sharp edges, and low quality settings create smudges and halos around letters.
Why does the converted image look different on my phone and my computer?
Screens display images at different sizes and pixel densities. A low-resolution JPG that looks acceptable on a small phone screen may look soft when viewed large on a desktop.

Conclusion

A blurry PDF to JPG conversion is rarely a mystery. It is usually resolution, quality, or the nature of the PDF itself. Check what kind of page you have, choose the resolution that fits it, and compare at 100% zoom before sharing.
