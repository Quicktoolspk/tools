Image Optimization

Why Your Image Gets Bigger After Converting It to JPG

9 min read Image formats & compression

Introduction

Have you ever converted a PNG image to JPG expecting a smaller file, only to discover that the new image is actually larger?

It seems strange because JPG is commonly associated with smaller image files. However, converting an image does not guarantee that its file size will decrease.

The result depends on several factors, including the type of image, its pixel dimensions, the compression settings used by the converter, and whether the original image contains transparency.

In this guide, you will learn why a JPG can become larger than its PNG source, how to identify the cause, and what to do when you need a smaller image for a website, online form, email, or social media post.

Quick answer: Why does your JPG become larger?

A JPG file can become larger than its PNG source for several reasons:

  • Different compression settings: The converter may save the JPG at a high quality setting with relatively little compression.
  • Image content: Photographs, screenshots, illustrations, and flat-color graphics respond differently to different compression methods.
  • Transparency handling: JPG does not support transparent pixels. Transparent areas must be replaced with a solid background when converting to JPG.
  • Pixel dimensions: If the converted image is larger in width or height, its file size may increase significantly.
  • Different export settings: Metadata, color profiles, and the particular encoder can affect the final file size.

The key lesson: Changing the file format is not the same as optimizing the image.

1. PNG and JPG handle images differently

To understand why a conversion can increase file size, it helps to understand how these formats store image information.

PNG

PNG uses lossless compression. It can preserve the original pixel information and support transparency. It is often useful for logos, interface graphics, and screenshots containing sharp text or lines.

Trade-off: some PNG images can be relatively large, especially when they contain detailed photographic content.

JPG / JPEG

JPG commonly uses lossy compression. It discards some image information to reduce file size, making it a popular choice for photographs.

Trade-off: aggressive compression can introduce blur, blockiness, or visible artifacts around text and sharp edges.

A photograph saved as PNG may become much smaller when converted to JPG. But a screenshot or graphic that PNG already compresses efficiently may not benefit in the same way.

2. The image type matters more than you might expect

One of the easiest mistakes is treating every image as if it were a photograph. Different types of images have different compression needs.

Image type What may happen when converted to JPG Practical recommendation
Photograph File size may decrease substantially Try JPG at a suitable quality setting
Transparent logo Transparent areas need a background Keep PNG if transparency is required
Screenshot with text File size may increase or text may look worse Compare PNG with a high-quality JPG
Flat illustration Results depend on colors and details Compare PNG, JPG, and WebP
Large image with many details High-quality JPG may remain large Resize first if the dimensions exceed your needs

These are general expectations, not guaranteed outcomes. The best format should be selected according to the image's purpose and the actual result.

3. Five checks to find the real cause

Before converting the image again, check these five things.

Check 1: Compare the original and converted dimensions

An image measuring 4000 × 3000 pixels contains far more pixels than one measuring 1200 × 900 pixels.

If a converter accidentally increases the dimensions during export, the output can become larger even when it uses JPG compression.

What to do:

  1. Check the original image width and height.
  2. Check the converted image width and height.
  3. Confirm that the output dimensions match your requirements.
  4. If the image is too large for its intended use, resize it before or during conversion.

For example, if you only need a product image displayed at 800 pixels wide, exporting a 4000-pixel-wide image may be unnecessary. The appropriate size depends on the intended display and any high-resolution requirements.

Check 2: Review the JPG quality setting

Many image converters let you choose a quality level. Higher quality generally preserves more visual detail but can produce a larger file.

If your converter exports JPG at a very high quality setting, the output may be larger than expected, especially if the source PNG is already efficiently compressed.

What to do:

  1. Export the same image at a high quality setting.
  2. Export it again at a medium-high setting.
  3. Compare both file sizes.
  4. Zoom in to inspect text, edges, and fine details.
  5. Choose the smallest output that still looks good for your intended use.

Do not choose a quality setting based on its number alone. Different encoders can produce different results at the same nominal setting.

Check 3: Find out whether the PNG contains transparency

PNG can store transparent areas, while JPG cannot.

Imagine a company logo with a transparent background. When you convert it to JPG, the transparent areas must be filled with a solid color, such as white.

This background change does not automatically make the file smaller. The final size still depends on the image content and compression settings.

What to do:

  • If you need a transparent logo, keep it as PNG or use another format that supports transparency, such as WebP.
  • If you need a JPG, choose the background color before converting.
  • Check the edges of the logo to make sure they look clean against the new background.

Important: Converting a transparent PNG to JPG will not preserve its transparency. Changing the JPG back to PNG later will not restore the original transparent pixels.

Check 4: Consider what is actually inside the image

A photograph contains many gradual changes in color and texture. A screenshot may contain large flat areas, small text, and sharp edges.

These differences affect how compression performs. PNG may already be efficient for certain graphics, while JPG may be more efficient for photographs.

What to do: Try comparing formats with the same source image rather than assuming JPG is always smaller. If your image is a graphic or screenshot, include PNG and WebP in your comparison when those formats are suitable for your workflow.

Check 5: Check export settings and metadata

Converters may use different encoders, color profiles, and metadata settings. These can affect the output size.

If your image dimensions and quality appear unchanged but the file is unexpectedly large, try another export setting or a trusted converter that provides clear control over quality and dimensions.

Avoid repeatedly converting a JPG into another JPG. Each lossy re-encoding can reduce image quality without guaranteeing a smaller file.

4. A simple troubleshooting decision tree

Use this quick guide to choose your next step.

  • Yes: Resize to the required dimensions and export again.
  • No: Check the quality setting and image type.

  • Yes: Compare PNG with other suitable formats before switching to JPG.
  • No: Test a lower JPG quality setting and compare visual quality.

  • Select the format and settings that meet your size, quality, and compatibility requirements.

6. How to reduce image size without ruining quality

If your goal is a smaller file, follow these steps in order.

Step 1: Choose the right format

Use JPG as a starting point for photographs. Keep PNG when transparency or crisp graphic details are important. Consider WebP when your target software or website supports it.

Step 2: Resize only when necessary

Reduce dimensions if the original image is larger than your actual use requires. Do not resize an image smaller than necessary for its intended display.

Step 3: Adjust compression gradually

Try a few quality settings rather than immediately choosing the lowest one. Inspect small text, edges, skin tones, and other important details.

Step 4: Compare the actual file size

Check the resulting file's size in KB or MB. Do not assume that a format or quality setting guarantees a particular file size.

Step 5: Keep the original file

Save a copy of the original before making lossy conversions. This lets you retry with different settings without repeatedly degrading the same image.

7. Common mistakes to avoid

  • Converting every image to JPG: This can remove transparency and make some graphics look worse.
  • Choosing the highest quality every time: The largest quality setting is not always necessary for web use.
  • Ignoring dimensions: An unnecessarily large image can remain heavy even after conversion.
  • Judging only by file size: A smaller image may look noticeably worse.
  • Converting repeatedly: Re-encoding lossy images can accumulate quality loss.
  • Assuming every converter behaves identically: Export settings and encoding implementations vary.
  • Deleting the original too soon: Keep the original until you have checked the converted file.

8. Frequently asked questions

Why is my JPG bigger than my PNG?
Your converter may be using high JPG quality, the PNG may already be efficiently compressed, or the output may have larger dimensions. Compare the dimensions and export settings first.
Does JPG always make images smaller?
No. JPG often reduces the size of photographs, but the result depends on image content, dimensions, compression settings, and the original file's characteristics.
Should I convert a transparent PNG logo to JPG?
Only if you no longer need transparency and are happy with a solid background. Otherwise, keep the PNG or consider another transparency-supporting format.
Is PNG better than JPG?
Neither format is best for every situation. PNG is useful for transparency and graphics with sharp edges. JPG is commonly useful for photographs where lossy compression can reduce file size.
Will converting JPG back to PNG restore image quality?
No. Saving a JPG as PNG does not recover information already discarded by JPG compression. The new PNG may even be larger.
How can I reduce a JPG file without changing its dimensions?
Try adjusting its quality setting and comparing the output. You may be able to reduce file size while keeping acceptable visual quality, but the result depends on the image and encoder.
Which format should I use for a website?
Choose based on the image's purpose, visual quality, file size, and compatibility needs. JPG, PNG, and WebP each have appropriate use cases. Test representative images rather than relying on a universal rule.

Conclusion

If converting PNG to JPG makes your image larger, do not assume the converter is broken. First check the pixel dimensions, compression settings, transparency, and type of image you are converting.

For photographs, JPG may be a good choice. For transparent logos and graphics with sharp text, PNG may be more appropriate. When file size matters, compare the actual output and inspect its quality before choosing a final format.

The best image is not simply the smallest one. It is the image that meets your size, quality, and compatibility requirements.
