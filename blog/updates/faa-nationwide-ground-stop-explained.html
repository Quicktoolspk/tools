FAA Nationwide Ground Stop: Why All US Flights Were Grounded and How It Affects Travelers

Last updated: November 13, 2026 8 min read Breaking News
Breaking News
Nationwide ground stop issued by the FAA

A major telecommunications outage forced the FAA to halt all departing US flights on November 13, 2026. Here is what you need to know.

When the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) halts air traffic across the entire United States, millions of travelers feel the ripple effects within minutes. On Friday, November 13, 2026, the FAA issued an extremely rare nationwide ground stop, ordering all departing flights at US airports to remain on the ground until further notice due to "extraordinary circumstances and for reasons of safety."

Direct Answer: A nationwide FAA ground stop is an emergency air traffic control measure that prevents any aircraft from taking off across the United States. The November 13, 2026 ground stop was triggered by a major telecommunications and frequency outage affecting all 22 regional Air Route Traffic Control Centers, making it only the third nationwide grounding in modern US aviation history.

Whether you are currently stuck at an airport terminal or trying to understand how national airspace safety works, this guide breaks down the exact cause of the November 2026 outage, what happens to planes already in the sky, and how to protect your travel plans.

What Happened During the November 13, 2026 FAA Ground Stop?

Shortly before 4:00 p.m. ET (20:54 UTC) on November 13, 2026, the FAA’s Air Traffic Control System Command Center posted an urgent advisory on its National Airspace System Status website:

"By order of the Federal Aviation Command Center all airports are ground stopped. All departing traffic should remain ground stopped until further notice. Inbound international flights will be managed on a case by case basis."

Moments before the nationwide order went into effect, localized ground stops had already been placed on Chicago’s O'Hare International Airport and Midway International Airport due to equipment failures.

The Root Cause: Air Route Traffic Control Center Outage

US Secretary of Transportation Sean Duffy confirmed that the stoppage was caused by a widespread communication issue impacting all Air Route Traffic Control Centers (ARTCCs).

  • What ARTCCs do: The United States relies on 22 regional ARTCC facilities spaced across the country. While airport towers manage takeoffs and landings, ARTCC controllers guide aircraft during the high-altitude "cruise" phase of their flights between cities.
  • No evidence of a cyberattack: Department of Homeland Security Director Markwayne Mullin stated in a press briefing that preliminary investigations pointed strictly to a technical telecommunications and frequency outage rather than a coordinated cyberattack or "nefarious" threat.

At the exact moment the order was issued, aviation tracking platform Flightradar24 recorded 4,042 flights airborne and destined for US airports. By the end of the day, nearly 7,000 flights across the country faced delays or cancellations.

How it works: Regional Air Route Traffic Control Centers (ARTCCs) manage high-altitude flights between local airport terminals. When their communication fails, planes cannot safely be passed from one sector to another.

What Does a "Ground Stop" Actually Mean?

Many travelers confuse a ground stop with a ground delay program or a complete airspace closure. Understanding the difference helps clarify why some planes keep flying while others sit at the gate.

  1. Ground Stop (Complete Departure Halt): The FAA prohibits planes destined for a specific airport—or all airports nationwide—from taking off. Planes already in the air are not forced to land immediately at the nearest field unless there is a security threat; instead, they safely continue to their destinations under controller guidance.
  2. Ground Delay Program (Traffic Metering): Rather than stopping all takeoffs, the FAA assigns delayed departure times to flights so planes arrive at a congested or weather-affected airport at a slower, manageable pace.
  3. Airspace Closure (Total Evacuation): Reserved for extreme national security emergencies, an airspace closure orders all airborne planes to land immediately and clears the skies completely (as seen on September 11, 2001).

How Often Has the FAA Grounded All US Flights?

A nationwide ground stop is one of the rarest events in commercial aviation. The November 2026 stoppage is only the third time in modern history that the FAA has halted all departing flights nationwide:

DateEvent / TriggerScope & Impact
September 11, 2001 Terrorist attacks in New York and Washington, D.C. Complete US airspace closure. All airborne flights were diverted or ordered to land immediately; departures halted for two days.
January 11, 2023 Failure of the FAA's NOTAM (Notice to Air Missions) pilot alert system Nationwide departure pause lasting roughly 90 minutes to two hours. Triggered over 10,000 flight delays and 1,300+ cancellations.
November 13, 2026 Telecommunications outage across all 22 Air Route Traffic Control Centers Nationwide departure halt for all US airports; inbound international flights managed case-by-case. Nearly 7,000 initial delays and cancellations.

(Note: In July 2024, major carriers like Delta, American, and United issued a global ground stop, though that was caused by a third-party CrowdStrike/Microsoft IT outage rather than an FAA air traffic control mandate.)

Why Ground Stops Cause Ripple Effects for Days

Even after FAA technicians restore communication systems and lift a ground stop, airports do not return to normal immediately. Aviation operates like a tightly connected chain:

  • Aircraft Misalignment: If a plane scheduled to fly from Chicago to Miami is grounded for three hours, its subsequent flights from Miami to Dallas and Dallas to Seattle are automatically delayed or canceled.
  • Crew Duty Limits: Pilots and flight attendants are strictly governed by federal safety laws limiting how many hours they can work in a single shift. Prolonged tarmac or gate delays often cause crews to "time out," forcing airlines to cancel flights until replacement crews arrive.
  • Gate Congestion: When departing planes cannot leave their gates, arriving flights have nowhere to park, creating gridlock on airport taxiways.

What Travelers Should Do During a Nationwide Ground Stop

If you are caught in an airport during an FAA ground stop, taking quick, organized steps can help you get rebooked ahead of the crowd:

  1. Check the Official FAA Status Page: Visit nasstatus.faa.gov for real-time updates on whether your departure or arrival airport is under an active ground stop or ground delay.
  2. Use the Airline App While Calling Support: Do not rely solely on the gate agent line. Open your airline's mobile app to check for automatic rebooking options while simultaneously calling the airline's customer service number (or international support line, which often has shorter wait times).
  3. Know Your Refund Rights: Under US Department of Transportation rules, if your flight is canceled—or significantly delayed and you choose not to travel—you are entitled to a full cash refund to your original payment method, even if you bought a non-refundable ticket. You do not have to accept travel vouchers.
  4. Ask About Hotel and Meal Vouchers (With Realistic Expectations): Because an FAA system outage is classified as an air traffic control issue outside an airline's direct control, carriers are not legally required to pay for hotels. However, many major airlines still issue meal vouchers or partner hotel discounts during widespread disruptions if you ask politely.
  5. Keep Digital Copies of Your Documents: Save screenshots of your boarding pass, flight delay notifications, and receipts for meals or hotels. If you booked your trip with a travel rewards credit card, trip delay insurance may reimburse your out-of-pocket expenses once a delay passes 6 to 12 hours. Quick Summary

  • What happened: On November 13, 2026, the FAA grounded all departing flights across the United States due to a telecommunications outage affecting all 22 Air Route Traffic Control Centers.
  • Airborne flights: Planes already in the air (over 4,000 at the time of the order) continued flying safely to their destinations, while inbound international flights were managed case-by-case.
  • Historical rarity: This marks only the third nationwide FAA ground stop in modern US history, following 9/11 (2001) and the NOTAM system failure (January 2023).
  • Passenger action: Monitor the FAA's National Airspace System website, rebook immediately via your airline's app, and know that canceled flights qualify for full cash refunds regardless of ticket type.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

1. Are planes forced to land immediately during an FAA ground stop?
No. During a standard ground stop, planes already airborne are allowed to continue safely to their scheduled destinations. Only aircraft that have not yet taken off are held on the ground. Immediate emergency diversions only occur during a full airspace closure.
2. How long does a nationwide FAA ground stop usually last?
Most localized ground stops last between 30 minutes and two hours. Nationwide ground stops depend entirely on how fast technicians resolve the underlying technical failure—for example, the January 2023 NOTAM outage lasted under two hours, whereas residual airline delays lasted into the following day.
3. Are international flights into the US canceled during a ground stop?
Not automatically. During the November 2026 ground stop, the FAA stated that inbound international flights already en route to the United States would be managed on a case-by-case basis. However, international flights that have not yet departed from foreign airports for the US may be held at their origin gates.
4. Was the November 2026 FAA ground stop caused by a cyberattack?
According to Department of Homeland Security Director Markwayne Mullin, preliminary investigations found no indication of a cyberattack or malicious interference. Officials attributed the stoppage to a technical telecommunications and frequency outage.
5. Can I get a refund if my flight is delayed because of an FAA ground stop?
If your airline cancels your flight, you are legally entitled to a full refund if you choose not to rebook. If your flight is delayed—even due to an FAA air traffic control issue—you are also entitled to a refund if the delay is "significant" (generally 3+ hours for domestic flights or 6+ hours for international flights) and you decide not to travel.
6. Where can I check live FAA airport ground stop updates?
You can check real-time ground stops, ground delay programs, and airport closures directly on the official FAA National Airspace System Status portal at nasstatus.faa.gov.

Conclusion

A nationwide FAA ground stop is a stark reminder of how heavily modern air travel relies on seamless communication between ground control centers and aircraft in the sky. While sitting through an indefinite delay at the terminal is frustrating, ground stops exist to ensure air traffic controllers never operate blind when critical communication frequencies fail. If your travel plans are affected, stay off the terminal customer service lines when possible, use your airline's mobile app to secure the earliest rebooking, and keep your receipts organized in case you qualify for travel insurance or airline compensation.

This article reflects reporting available on November 13, 2026. It should be updated as the FAA issues new advisories and airlines confirm changes to their schedules.
